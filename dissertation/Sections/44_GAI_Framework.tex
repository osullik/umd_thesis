\section{Abstract Framework}
\label{section:44-GAI-Framework}

The \gai benchmark framework is presented below and summarized in figure~\ref{figure:gai-framework}. 
The framework is the overarching term given to the collection of \gai problems that comprise the benchmark. 
Each problem is separately designed and implemented with its own task definitions and datasets. 
The approaches represent an implementation on a SUT that is attempting the problem.

This section will present each of the steps in our proposed benchmark development methodology that is summarized in figure~\ref{figure:framework}. 
We focus our descriptions on how the questions in the bottom quarter of each step generate the expected outputs from each step, and how the transition questions in the grey circles should inform the designer's thinking. 
The diagram also shows which components of the benchmark framework the outputs of each step directly contribute to. 

We present the key methodological considerations at each step to develop \textit{problem} to be added to the benchmarks and make some general observations about the requirement for and design of the observation apparatus. 
Chapter~\ref{chapter:50-Community-Detection-Case-Study} demonstrates a worked example of how the methodology is implemented for a specific problem.

\textbf{Identifying the Use Case}
The first step in creating the benchmark step is to identify the underlying domain for the benchmark and select several problem use cases. 
The use case may be driven by necessity, or the target of exploratory research.
The domain should be from the real world, but provided there are open problems, and some representative datasets are available, most problems can for formalized into a use-case.

Characterizing the problem is about identifying what it is that we want to test, and identifying the tasks and datasets typical of the domain with a background study. 
Tasks should be defined as general goals and challenges rather than specific \textit{kernels}.
The generality subtly encourages formulating real problems into tasks instead of abstracting them to a kernel lacking context and also encourages innovative approaches. 
The intent is to shift the focus from racing optimized implementations against each other to figuring out how to best trade off time and accuracy
For example in \gai, a problem definition might be \textit{Given the input X, identify who the immediate associates of a person with name Y, provide the output as a list of vertex IDs} rather than \textit{BFS to node X}, \textit{search for X in a GDB} etc.

\textbf{Identifying and Characterizing the Phenomena of Interest.}
The goal of characterizing the phenomena is to identify what it is that we want to understand about the problems, and what metrics we need to collect towards understanding it. 
The phenomena of interest is the trade-off between the efficiency, effectiveness and cost of \gai systems.
Thus, the metrics that should be selected for the benchmark should characterize the trade-off between those aspects of performance. 
While not shown explicitly in figure~\ref{figure:gai-framework}, the phenomena are generated by the interactions of the approaches, datasets and tasks within a given problem and collected by the observation
For example, in most \gai tasks we expect \textit{ETL Time, Execution Time, Batch Latency\footnote{\textnormal{\scriptsize The time between when a vertex or edge is added to a graph and when it appears in the result of a workload~\cite{Basak2020}}}} and \textit{Cost}. 
For each specific problem, the phenomena must be tailored so that the assessed `goodness' of an output is meaningful.

\textbf{Construct the Investigation Apparatus.}

Constructing the investigation apparatus has three key steps.
First is collecting or generating datasets that will stress each hypothesis to stimulate the phenomena we intend to observe. 
The second is defining the metrics of interest and developing a mechanism to collect the data needed to generate them. 
The third step is integrating the SUT into the apparatus
In an Input/Output paradigm, this will just be some API configurations, but for a hosted solution may involve test compilations etc. 
Specific to the \gai benchmark is a need to trade-off between effectiveness and efficiency that will require sampling the available answers over time, and evaluating correctness as a distance from the optimal solution, rather than a binary judgement of correctness.
