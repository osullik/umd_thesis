\section{Proposed \gai Benchmark Framework}
\label{section:44-GAI-Framework}

This section will present each of the steps in our proposed benchmark development methodology summarized in Figure~\ref{figure:gai-framework-overview-ch4}, showing how we combine Hooker's methodology for the empirical study of algorithms with Goldratt's constraint theory to develop a `grey-box' approach to \gai benchmarking using an Input/Output model.
It should be used as a guide for applying the methodology to develop a single \gai benchmark problem to be inserted into the framework. 
The Figure consists of six steps across the six black boxes. 
The grey circles are the focusing questions for each step, and the questions below the lines in the black boxes are the output prompts that define what the step should be producing. 
The arrows show how each step contributes to the benchmark framework. 
Note that the \gai benchmark design is independent of the \ac{SUT}, instead defining the observation apparatus as a single interaction point between the \gai benchmark framework and the \ac{SUT}.
We present the critical methodological considerations at each step to develop \textit{problem} to be added to the benchmarks and make some general observations about the requirement for and design of the observation apparatus. 
Chapter~\ref{chapter:50-Community-Detection-Case-Study} demonstrates a worked example of how the methodology is implemented for a specific problem.

\textbf{Identifying the Use Case.}
The first step in creating the benchmark step is to identify the underlying domain for the benchmark and select several problem use cases. 
The use case may be driven by necessity or the target of exploratory research.
The domain should be from the real world, but provided there are open problems and some representative datasets are available, most problems can be formalized into a use case.

\textbf{Identifying and Characterizing a Relevant Problem.}
Characterizing the problem is about identifying what we want to test and identifying the tasks and datasets typical of the domain with a background study. 
Tasks should be defined as general goals and challenges rather than specific \textit{kernels}.
The generality subtly encourages formulating problems into tasks instead of abstracting them to a kernel lacking context and also encourages innovative approaches. 
For example, in \gai, a problem definition might be \textit{`Given the input X, identify who the immediate associates of a person with name Y, provide the output as a list of vertex IDs'} rather than \textit{`BFS to node X'}, \textit{`search for X in a GDB'} etc.
The problem-centric approach still allows for comparing like-approaches (e.g., comparing the same BFS algorithm on two different SUTs) by freezing all other benchmark components and varying the SUT hardware. 

\textbf{Identifying and Characterizing the Phenomena of Interest.}
The goal of characterizing the phenomena is to identify what we want to understand about the problems and what metrics we need to collect to understand them. 
The phenomena of interest is the trade-off between the efficiency, effectiveness, and cost of \gai systems.
Thus, the metrics selected for the benchmark should characterize the trade-off between those aspects of performance. 
While not shown explicitly in Figure~\ref{figure:gai-framework-overview-ch4}, the phenomena are generated by the interactions of the approaches, datasets, and tasks within a given problem and collected by the observation.
For example, in most \gai tasks, we expect \textit{ETL Time, Execution Time, Batch Latency\footnote{\textnormal{\scriptsize The time between when a vertex or edge is added to a graph and when it appears in the result of a workload~\cite{Basak2020}}}} and \textit{Cost} to be the metrics. 
For each problem, the phenomena must be tailored so that an output's assessed `goodness' is meaningful.

\textbf{Conduct Exploratory Experimentation.}
Exploratory experimentation provides the initial clues about where the bottlenecks, friction points, and curiosities are that are worthy of further study. 
We are trying to understand how the phenomena relate to the problems and develop theories that we can explore formally later in the design process. 
Experimentation can be direct, using the problems and datasets identified to test some simple theories about expected performance.
It can also be indirect, relying on a synthesis of prior experimental work to develop an understanding of the expected phenomena arising from a given approach to a particular problem.
In \gai, reviews of workload analyses, the findings of prior benchmarks, and the designs of acceleration techniques offer valuable insight into what approaches will interact with what data to produce interesting phenomena.

\textbf{Develop Hypotheses.}
Developing hypotheses is a scientific approach to quantifying the behavior we expect to observe in the system and designing circumstances to stimulate those behaviors. 
Hypotheses are developed in this methodology by using the knowledge gathered during exploratory experimentation.
Hypotheses can be anything, but it can be helpful to derive them by applying the three constraint levers of time (efficiency), resources (cost), and accuracy (effectiveness). 
These levers provide a mechanism to identify the trade-offs in \gai between efficiency, effectiveness, and cost by constraining one (or more) and observing changes in the phenomena under observation.
Table~\ref{table:gai-variance} summarizes the behavior we expect to stimulate from a SUT by constraining time, accuracy, or cost across any \gai problem and defines an important interaction of constraint theory with the empirical approach to the study of algorithms, showing how manipulating the input or the constraints on efficiency, effectiveness and cost can shift the stress points within a \ac{SUT}.

\noindent Hypotheses should be formally specified in the form:

{\centering \textit{"When [lever] is constrained for [task] on [dataset], we expect [change] in [phenomena]"}}

\textbf{Construct the Investigation Apparatus.}
The investigation apparatus is constructed in three key steps.
First is collecting or generating datasets that will stress each hypothesis to stimulate the phenomena we intend to observe. 
The second is defining the metrics of interest and developing a mechanism to collect the data needed to generate them. 
The third step is integrating the SUT into the apparatus.
There will be some API configurations in an input/output paradigm, but for a hosted solution, it may involve test compilations, etc. 
Specific to the \gai benchmark is a need to trade-off between effectiveness and efficiency that will require sampling the available answers over time and evaluating correctness as a distance from the ground-truth solution rather than a binary judgment of correctness. 

\textbf{Analyze the Results, Developing a Theoretic Explanation and Iterating.}
These three steps are combined because they are largely beyond the scope of the benchmark itself. 
The researcher is responsible for these; the benchmark must support them in the following steps. 
To that end, the benchmark should report the \textit{efficiency, effectiveness} and \textit{cost} (performance metrics) of each workload on each system under test configuration. 
It should also report the details of the datasets, implementations, and SUT for posterity.
Results should be viewable from the perspective of a SUT (exploring each aspect of performance) and aspect (comparing across SUTs).
The preliminary results can then be used to develop specific constraint-based experiments to be incorporated in future benchmark iterations as the hypotheses are refined.

\section{Summary: A `Grey-Box' \gai Benchmark Framework}
This chapter develops the \gai Benchmark framework by identifying the common benchmark components and the need to add an `observation apparatus' to mediate between benchmark problems and a \ac{SUT} in an Input/Output paradigm. 
Synthesizing the empirical approach for the study of algorithms with constraint theory shows how our \gai benchmark problem development methodology empowers a benchmark developer to identify the problems and datasets that will trigger known and suspected bottlenecks in \gai algorithms and systems, creating a `grey-box' method to investigate the internal state of \ac{SUT}s without the invasive access required by traditional `white-box' approaches. 
The next chapter shows how the \gai benchmark problem development methodology creates the \textit{Population Segmentation Benchmark Problem} for the \gai Benchmark Framework and how the benchmark can evaluate systems' relative performance.
