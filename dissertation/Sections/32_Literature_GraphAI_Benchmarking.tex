\textbf{Graph task coverage is too abstract for real-world \gai performance assessment.}
The most commonly benchmarked \gai tasks are \textit{Breadth First Search} (BFS), \textit{Single Source Shortest Path} (SSSP), \textit{Weakly Connected Components} (WCC), \textit{Mean Local Clustering Coefficient} (MLCC) and \textit{PageRank} (PR) which represent simple kernels.
%
\footnote{In benchmarking, the smallest measurable `thing' is an \textit{atomic operation} (e.g. traversing an edge). 
Multiple atomic operations compose into a \textit{kernel} (e.g. BFS), which can compose into \textit{toy, synthetic} or \textit{real} programs, which implement kernels to solve some higher level problem at varying levels of scale and realism~\cite{Saavedra1996}. See section~\ref{section:42-GAI-Methodology} for further detail.}.
%
The most commonly given reason for selecting these kernel tasks is that they are foundational to graph processing. 
Decomposing a complicated program to primitive kernels is useful for characterizing which specific program components are bottlenecks~\cite{Saavedra1996}, but since there is no commonly agreed primitive building blocks for graph processing (section~\ref{section:25-graph-primitives}), a fair comparison between approaches is difficult at the kernel level only~\cite{Hennessy2019} and a more comprehensive \gai assessment must compose more complicated programs.

\textbf{Graph Manipulations are under-supported and their absence does not reflect real-world \gai processing.}
Only nine of the surveyed benchmarks explicitly support graph manipulations, reflecting the static-graph processing assumption that the graph will only be built once and the time and cost will be amortized over the life of the graph.
As the survey of \gai tasks from section~\ref{section:11-graph-based-ai} shows, the static-graph assumption is not valid in any real-world application.
As a result, the limited coverage of graph manipulations makes it difficult to generalize their results to dynamic graph processing. 
Furthermore, only three benchmarks (SAGA-Bench~\cite{Basak2020}, SRBench~\cite{Zhang2012} and PAGNFF~\cite{Wu2021a}) provide explicit support to streaming graph tasks. 
Without understanding the efficiency and cost of graph manipulation operations, it is difficult to make informed judgments about the trade-offs of between \gai implementations and environments. 

\textbf{Approximate and incremental \gai approaches are not well covered.}
The current benchmark landscape only offers implicit support to incremental and approximate algorithms. 
In the real-world setting where time and other resources may be constrained, tradeoffs between the effectiveness and the efficiency or cost of a \gai approach may need to be made. 
The scale and velocity of graph data now and into the future require methods to process streaming graphs and support approximate or incremental approaches to solving \gai problems~\cite{Regli2022a}. 
The lack of coverage highlights that these approaches have not been a focus, and that additional work is needed to increase their use towards creating levers to pull to trade-off effectiveness and efficency.

\textbf{Datasets of diverse sizes and shapes are available, but popularity varies.}
The sizes of available datasets varies significantly. 
The maximal size of datasets varies from up to $70$ trillion edges in Graph500~\cite{Murphy2010} down to $20$ thousand edges~\cite{Huang2021}.
The huge Graph500 graph is over $1$ Pb uncompressed, more than capable of stressing high-end commercial and research systems, with the smallest dataset being easily able to fit in the memory of most personal computers. 
Diverse topologies are available from the Stanford Large Network Dataset Collection providing over 120 real-world graphs from 23 different domains~\cite{Leskovec2014}, including multiple different mesh and scale-free sources.
The popularity of datasets varies with time.
When a new `state of the art' dataset emerges, benchmarks will flock to include it for a while, and then eventually switch to the new hotness when a new dataset emerges. 
The key problem with static real-world datasets is that they can quickly become an optimization target, rather than a measure of performance, leading to implementations and environments that are overfitted to the benchmark problems~\cite{Hooker1994,Hooker1995}.

\textbf{Heterogeneous graphs are not well covered.}
Being able to process complex heterogeneous graphs consistent with knowledge graphs is reflective of the expected input data for \gai in the real world~\cite{Regli2022a}
Our survey shows that the only focus area that consistently uses heterogeneous graphs in its evaluations is GDB benchmarking. 
While GNNs implicitly use attributed graphs, they tend to be simple, with homogeneous node and edge types.
Traditional graph analytics very rarely use attributes because their focus is topological. 
As a result, there is not a widely used heterogeneous knowledge graph dataset, the public availability of resources like DBPedia~\cite{Auer2007} mitigate the impact.

\textbf{Dynamic graph datasets are very uncommon.}

Most of the benchmarks have at least one random and one scale-free graph in their collections, but mesh graphs and dynamic graphs appear in a minority of surveyed benchmarks. 
The lack of mesh graphs will lead to an absence of information about how \gai implementations and environments behave on graphs with large diameters and regular, low degrees. 
No explicit support for dynamic graphs means that existing graphs either have to be artificially transformed into dynamic graphs by randomly sampling nodes and edges and injecting them in batches or need to be sourced by experimenters.
Much like synthetic graph generation, artificially transforming a graph into a streaming graph loses information about patterns in vertex and edge creation, updates and deletion and provides a sub-optimal substitute. 
Sourcing of data by experimenters generally leads to the creation of synthetic data, or to the use of proprietary data which then is not released for comparative studies.

\subsection{Metric Coverage.}
The metrics generated by \gai benchmarks are the lenses through which we understand the relative effectiveness, efficiency and cost of \gai systems.
We survey $23$ graph benchmarks using published results and public leaderboards for their coverage of metrics and present the results in table~\ref{table:benchmark-metrics}.
The table groups the benchmarks by their focus areas (graph analytics, GDBs and GNNs), and summarizes the reported metrics per column. 
A benchmark is said to report a metric if any single workload within the benchmark reports that metric.

\textbf{Extract, Transform and Load timings are neglected.}
There is a strong preference for using kernel-level execution times, generally excluding any measurement of the time taken to \textit{extract} the data, \textit{transform} it into a graph format and \textit{load} (ETL) it into memory. 
Execution time is important and the most honest measure of a system's performance~\cite{Hennessy2019}.
However, the lack of ETL timings reflects the assumption that a graph is preconstructed and held in memory, which is not reflective of the dynamic graphs that \gai operates on in the real world~\cite{Regli2022a}.

\textbf{Throughput measures can be misleading.}
Throughput metrics are generally \textit{estimated} by counting the edges in the input graph, estimating the traversals required by a single iteration of the algorithm and multiplying that by the number of iterations to get the total edge traversals, which is then divided by the execution time to measure traversals per second~\cite{Bader2009, Intel2020}.
The drawback of this estimation strategy is that throughput becomes difficult to estimate for algorithms that aggregate or otherwise change the structure of the graph each iteration. 
One option is to implement traversal counters in code to track how often a traversal occurs, or operation counting can be implemented with a more invasive profiling approach. 
Counters rely on the person implementing the algorithm implementing them correctly. 
Profiling approaches can degrade execution time, and require access to a system beyond the Input/Output level typical of most benchmarks to implement. 
In either case, these solutions are suboptimal for calculating throughput and may indicate that throughput is not a particularly useful metric for evaluating the tradeoffs between efficiency, effectiveness and cost.
Further, a high throughput on a kernel task does not necessarily correlate to better efficiency, effectiveness or cost, as high throughput on an inefficient algorithm may be less performant than a lower throughput on a more efficient algorithm.

\textbf{Collecting detailed metrics is invasive and drives the Input/Output model of benchmarking.}
To collect interesting and useful metrics about the internal state of the \gai system (compute, memory, network and power), the system under test needs to be under the control of the benchmark owner so that the code can be correctly instrumented and the underlying system sampled throughout execution. 
As a result, these `internal' metrics are only observed in detailed workload analysis studies where the researcher has access beyond the inputs and outputs to the system and what can be externally observed. 
The typical inputs are a graph and some task to complete (the workload), the output is usually a (verifiable) solution to the task and the only variable that can be reliably externally observed is time. 
The Input/Output model for benchmarking is flexible, but does not facilitate the discovery of bottlenecks, which is critical for understanding which parts of the system should be improved.

\textbf{Effectiveness is neglected}
Of the surveyed benchmarks, many lacked effectiveness metrics.
The lack of \textit{effectiveness} metrics reflects that many of the graph problems are deterministic, and so for the other metrics to be admissible the output must be correct and reporting an accuracy of $1.0$ is unnecessary. 
On the other hand, benchmarks for newer problems like \textit{Long Range Graph Learning}~\cite{Dwivedi2022} often only include effectiveness metrics, ignoring time and space efficiency until the problem can be effectively solved.

\textbf{There is a need for an Algorithmic Development Maturity Model}
There is a need for a maturity model to describe the growth of a field from `proving it works' to `proving it works effectively' to `proving it works efficiently'.
A review of maturity model design processes highlights that the design of a maturity model requires rigorous design and experimentation and that the field is plagued by half-baked models~\cite{Lahrmann2011, Sadiq2021}. 
We see the benefit in developing a maturity model for algorithmic development but believe it needs to be wider in scope than the \gai focus of this thesis and so leave the construction of the maturity model for future work.

The missing dimension is cost, which can be estimated using a proxy like power consumption, but is difficult to implement without direct access to the system under test.
