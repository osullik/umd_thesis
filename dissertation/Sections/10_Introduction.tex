\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chapter:10-Introduction}
Graph data structures and the algorithms that operate on them are foundational to computer science.
As the social media platforms of the early $21^{st}$ century have proliferated into the global consciousness, graphs have been increasingly used as powerful abstractions to represent interconnected data collected from the real world. 
Recent years have seen a `Cambrian explosion' of artificial intelligence (AI) applications, and the hardware accelerators used to improve their efficiency~\cite{Regli2022b}.
Tasks that have traditionally been in the domain of graph processing are being challenged by modern machine-learning approaches to graphs.
We term the overlap of graph processing and AI and \gai.

For \gai to be useful in the real world, systems must be capable of processing massive, heterogeneous, dynamic graphs under resource constraints. 
Maximizing performance given limited resources involves making an informed tradeoff between these factors, which requires having a mechanism for evaluating \gai systems under varying conditions.


Benchmarks are widely used, effective tools to compare the relative performance of implementations and the environments that they run in. 
However, they tend to manifest as platforms for `racing' systems against each other rather than tools for scientific inquiry.
Developing a comprehensive benchmark requires a rigorous framework, which is the goal of this thesis.

The remainder of Chapter~\ref{chapter:10-Introduction} will describe \gai, illustrating the taxonomy we propose using a study of \gai in the \textit{Intelligence and Security} (I\&S) domain.
The chapter concludes by formulating the problem of how to measure the tradeoffs in effectiveness and efficiency in \gai systems.
