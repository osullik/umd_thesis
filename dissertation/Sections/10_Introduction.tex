\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chapter:10-Introduction}
Graph data structures and the algorithms that operate on them are foundational to computer science.
As the social media platforms of the early $21^{st}$ century have proliferated into the global consciousness, graphs have increasingly been used as powerful abstractions to represent interconnected data collected from the real world. 
Recent years have seen a `Cambrian explosion' of artificial intelligence (AI) applications, and the hardware accelerators used to improve their efficiency~\cite{Regli2022b}.
Tasks that have traditionally been in the domain of graph processing are being challenged by modern, neural approaches to machine learning on graphs.
We term the overlap of graph processing and AI and \gai.

For \gai to be useful in the real world, its implementations, and the environments they run in must be capable of processing \textit{massive}, \textit{heterogeneous}, \textit{dynamic} graphs under \textit{resource-constraints}. 
As a result, understanding the efficiency \& effectiveness, and the tradeoff between them in \gai is critical.

The remainder of Chapter~\ref{chapter:10-Introduction} will describe \gai, use a study of the \textit{Intelligence and Security} (I\&S) domain to illustrate its importance in the real world and provide an overview of the challenges in understanding and improving \gai.
The chapter concludes by formulating the problem that this thesis studies, namely, how to measure the tradeoffs in effectiveness and efficiency in \gai systems.
