\section{Conclusion}
\label{section:62-Conclusion}

\acresetall

This thesis develops a benchmarking framework for \gai. 
Chapter \ref{chapter:20-Preliminaries} identifies \gai as a growing important field with the emergence of concepts like model transparency, explainability, and interpretability.
It characterizes a taxonomy of \gai covering classical \ac{AI} problems in searching and planning through structural analysis, reasoning, and the development of \ac{GNN} and explainable \ac{AI}. 
We show how, for the Intelligence and Security domain, \gai approaches can address many of their problems and that deploying them to the real world imposes temporal, financial, \ac{SWaP}, and accuracy constraints. 
%
Frequently, one system will be expected to solve many \gai problems, so determining which system provides the best balance of efficiency, effectiveness, and cost across its problem sets is an essential question in procurement. 
The go-to tool for understanding the relative performance of systems is benchmarking. 
%
% However, the proliferation of distinct graph hardware, representation, and implementation acceleration techniques makes a `like-for-like' benchmarking comparison hard. 
% Viewed together with the heavily data-dependent character of \gai tasks and the ineffectiveness of modern general-purpose architectural optimizations for graph processing, \gai becomes very difficult to evaluate using traditional `black-box' benchmarking approaches and `white-box' workload analyses.
However, \gai is challenging to evaluate fairly using traditional `black-box' benchmarking approaches and `white-box' workload analyses because of the variety of graph abstraction models, representations, storage formats, primitive operations, and programming frameworks. 
Further, \gai tasks are heavily data-dependent, and modern general-purpose architectural optimizations are ineffective for graph processing computation, making evaluation even more challenging.

From Chapter \ref{chapter:30-Literature-Review}, we identify that a useful benchmark needs to do more than just `race' systems against each other on a leaderboard and introduce empirical and constraint-based benchmarking as alternate approaches that produce more meaningful results. 
Reviewing existing benchmarks shows a lack of coverage for dynamic graph processing. 
They focus on kernel-level tasks at the expense of a holistic `problem-centric' view and on either efficiency or effectiveness, never both. 
We show how prior workload analyses and efforts to accelerate \gai hardware provide detailed insights into the internal states of \gai systems and conclude that while the instrumentation and profiling required make these approaches infeasible for a large-scale benchmark, the data dependence of \gai tasks means we can manipulate the input to stimulate some of the same behaviors in our proposed `grey-box' approach to benchmarking. 

The findings from these three Chapters yield the \gai benchmark framework in Chapter~\ref{chapter:40-Graph-AI-Benchmarking}.
The problem-centric framework provides a shared interface between \gai benchmark problems and \ac{SUT}.
The framework design and benchmark problem development methodology are applied to the Population Segmentation problem from the \ac{IandS} domain as an exemplar use case in Chapter~\ref{chapter:50-Community-Detection-Case-Study}. 
By stepping through the benchmark problem development methodology, we develop the Population Segmentation Benchmark for the \gai benchmark framework. 

In addition to the methodological contribution, we describe the development of a tool to convert geospatial data from OpenStreetMaps to directed, weighted, attributed graphs with ground-truth community labels, filling a gap in publicly available mesh graphs for community detection.
We also provide a prototype implementation of the observation apparatus and run experiments showing how an engineer could apply it to collect evidence about which system architectures will meet their \gai use cases, informing hardware procurement decisions.
Analyzing the results of those experiments shows that the ability to trade off the effectiveness of a solution for a more efficient one is dependent on the existence of incremental or approximate algorithms that improve effectiveness monotonically over time and that \ac{GPU} can achieve a $172\times$ speedup over a baseline \ac{CPU} based system. 

This thesis shows that a transparent methodology, grounded in prior work, can develop a benchmark that applies a `grey-box' approach to evaluating a \ac{SUT} without needing to advance beyond the Input/Output boundary. 
It shows that the `gray-box' approach can estimate \gai performance on real-world tasks for analysts empirically by identifying the combinations of hardware, graph representations, and algorithms that will balance the performance of \gai problems. 
The formal methodology gives a clear path for adding other \gai problems to the benchmark in addition to Population segmentation. 
It promotes a transparent, reproducible approach to the scientific inquiry that is desperately needed to continue advancing \gai.
