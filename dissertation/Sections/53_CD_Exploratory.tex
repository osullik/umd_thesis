For our exploratory analysis, we conduct a survey of prior work in \gai benchmarking, workload analysis, and acceleration for the population segmentation problem, grouped broadly by approach (Table~\ref{Table:cd-survey}).

Almost all prior work falls within the `community detection' approach discussed in Section~\ref{section:51-CD-Problem}, and so the language of this section reflects that focus.
For each approach, we identify the reported worst-case complexity for the algorithm as the efficiency bottleneck and factors that degrade the quality of communities as the effectiveness bottlenecks. 

Below, we characterize our observations of the stressors to community detection performance: datasets, algorithmic weaknesses, modularity optimization flaws, and dynamic graph processing challenges.
During our exploration, we identified two techniques to improve performance: external information and approximate solutions.

\subsection{Datasets Observations}

\textbf{Graph size.}
From the survey in  Table~\ref{Table:cd-survey}, the worst-case time complexity is quadratic in the number of vertices, particularly for the dominant algorithms like Louvain~\cite{Blondel2008} and Leiden~\cite{Traag2019}.
We observe that in many cases, efficiency decreases as $|E|\gg|V|$. 

\textbf{Graph shape.}
Further, we identify that the effectiveness of community detection approaches degrades when the communities are `fuzzy' rather than `cliquey' and when the mean degree of the graph is high.

\subsection{Implementation Observations}

\textbf{Algorithmic weaknesses exist in community detection.}
Many approaches require the number of communities, clusters, or partitions to be known in advance, or they must run an algorithm like K-Means multiple times to discover the optimal number of communities~\cite{Kernighan1970, Reichardt2004, Ng2001, White2005, Yang2016, Wang2017}, significantly increasing the computational cost when the number of communities is large. 
Knowing the number of communities a priori does not reflect the discovery process expected in the real world~\cite{Regli2022a}.
Recursive-bisection partitioning approaches fail when there are three equally sized communities at a given recursion level; at least one will be mistakenly grouped with another and will usually be split across both. 
Greedy-agglomerative clustering approaches fail when two cliques are connected by nodes of a lower degree than the clique, pulling the `bridge' nodes into the cluster they bridge to rather than their own. 
Most approaches cannot assign a vertex to multiple communities because of the underlying assumptions about clustering and partitioning, so overlapping communities rely on graph attributes for effective community detection on heterogeneous graph structures~\cite{Reichardt2006}.
The most popular implementation (Louvain~\cite{Blondel2008}) has been shown to produce disconnected communities when the shape of the input graph contains hierarchical community structures that contain cliques joined by bridge nodes~\cite{Traag2019}.

\textbf{Modularity optimization is not a reliable strategy.}
Modularity has several problems despite being the dominant metric in measuring community goodness. 
Modularity optimization is $NP$-Complete~\cite{Brandes2006}, so algorithms must take heuristic approaches to apply it. 
Modularity is also scale-dependent, and optimization techniques suffer from a scaling factor where a community with a density of internal edges of the order of the $\sqrt[]{2|E|}$ times the number of edges in the graph will not be resolved.
That is, small communities will be missed by modularity optimization.
In general, `loosely connected' communities are mistakenly combined, especially when the community size distribution has long tails~\cite{Fortunato2007}.

\textbf{Dynamic processing relies on short batch latency.} 
The inputs to dynamic graph processing algorithms are either a collection of related graphs describing the same problem at several time slices or may have a stream of updates to reflect when a vertex is added, deleted, or modified its properties.
Consequently, the task is typically to identify when a community changes or which communities persist for extended periods (i.e., the co-traveler problem).
Many dynamic processing approaches batch the updates and apply a static community detection approach at each iteration, then calculate the similarity in communities between each timestep, with more efficient implementations keeping the prior state in memory to reduce computation~\cite{Rossetti2018}.
These batching approaches fail in real-time settings where the batch latency exceeds the length of each timestep $T$.

\textbf{Community Detection is suitable for approximate and incremental approaches.}
Clustering, partitioning, and \ac{CD} are typically implemented as iterative algorithms that converge to a heuristically optimal solution but can return some information at each iteration step and are vulnerable to local maxima.
Running an approximate algorithm may provide a less accurate result in a shorter timeframe, and iterative approaches that yield incremental improvements as time proceeds can take longer to converge than approaches that use a single-shot approach.
Datasets with hierarchical communities can have correct solutions at different resolution levels, meaning that an iterative algorithm can yield communities at different resolutions over time. 

\textbf{Weights and attributes are critical for segmenting fuzzy and overlapping communities.} 
Graph partitioning, clustering, and community detection are traditionally topology-only algorithms. 
However, access to some states, like the sentiment of an edge~\cite{Yang2007}, or the weights associated with edges~\cite{Rosvall2008} yield superior segments. 
The same is true of attributes, and prior knowledge about group membership of some nodes dramatically improves the identification of overlapping and fuzzy communities~\cite{Zhang2013}. 

\subsection{Outputs}
Completing the exploratory analysis through a literature review reveals the bottlenecks that impact population segmentation approaches, guiding hypothesis development in the next step. 

\textbf{Efficiency bottlenecks.}
We expect that increasing the number of vertices and the number of edges will degrade the efficiency of population segmentation approaches, given the worst-case complexity estimates in  Table~\ref{Table:cd-survey}.

\textbf{Effectiveness bottlenecks.} 
The effectiveness of \ac{CD} approaches is vulnerable to fuzzy and overlapping communities, denser graphs, and the existence of `bridges' between cliquey structures.

\textbf{Friction points.} 
We identify additional friction points, including the requirement for a priori knowledge of the number of communities by many approaches, the lack of support for overlapping communities, and the lack of work in dynamic \ac{CD} benchmarks.
