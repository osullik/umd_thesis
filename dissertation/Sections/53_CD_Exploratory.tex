\subsection{Datasets Observations}

\textbf{Graph Size.}
From the survey in table~\ref{table:cd-survey} we observe that in general, the worst-case complexity is at least quadratic in the number of vertices, particularly for the dominant algoreithms like Louvain~\cite{Blondel2008} and Leiden~\cite{Traag2019}.
We can conclude that the number of vertices in the graph will be a driver of efficiency degradation in almost all cases, with many of the approaches vulnerable to dense graph structures where $|E|\gg|V|$.

\textbf{Graph Shape.}
Further, we identify that the effectiveness of community detection approaches degrades, unsurprisingly, when the communities are `fuzzy' rather than `cliquey', and when the mean degree of the graph is high.

Many approaches require the number of communities/clusters/partitions to be known in advance or must run an algorithm like K-Means multiple times to discover the optimal number of communities (\cite{Kernighan1970, Reichardt2004, Ng2001,White2005, Yang2016, Wang2017}), significantly increasing the computational \nrscomment{cost} when there are many small communities \nrscomment{the small communities are not reflective or the computational cost is not reflective? theres subject-object disagreement or you should replace 'and' with another word} and not reflective of discovery process we expect to encounter in the real world~\cite{Regli2022a}.

Greedy Agglomerative approaches fail when two cliques are connected by nodes of low degree compared to the clique, pulling the `bridge' nodes into the cluster that they bridge to rather than their own. 
Most approaches are not capable of assigning a vertex to multiple communities because of the underlying assumptions about clustering and partitioning, \nrscomment{not clear how a highlights b. how are they related?} highlighting the importance of graph attributes to effective community detection on heterogeneous graph structures~\cite{Reichardt2006}.
The most popular implementation (Louvain \nrscomment{cite}) has been shown to produce disconnected communities when the shape of the input graph contains hierarchical community structures that contain cliques joined by bridge nodes~\cite{Traag2019}.

\textbf{Modularity Optimization.} \nrscomment{more specific header- should sound like an observation the way algo weaknesses does}
Modularity has several problems, despite being the dominant metric. 
Optimizing modularity is $NP$-Complete~\cite{Brandes2006}, so algorithms using modularity as the metric must take heuristic approaches to improve it. 
It is not scale-independent, that is, a scaling factor exists where a community with a density of internal edges of the order of the $\sqrt[]{2|E|}$ times the edges in the graph will not be resolved by modularity optimization. 
These 'loosely connected' communities are instead mistakenly combined, with the problem most prevalent when the distribution of community sizes have long tails~\cite{Fortunato2007}.

\textbf{Weights and Attributes.} \nrscomment{more specific header- should sound like an observation the way algo weaknesses does}
Graph partitioning, clustering, and community detection are traditionally topology-only algorithms. 
However, access to some states, like the sentiment of an edge~\cite{Yang2007}, or the weights associated with edges~\cite{Rosvall2008} yield superior segments.  
The same is true of utilizing attributes, and prior knowledge about group membership of some nodes greatly improves the identification of overlapping and fuzzy communities~\cite{Zhang2013}. \nrscomment{all the rest of the observations were things that degrade performance-- wasnt expecting this}

\textbf{Dynamic Processing.} \nrscomment{more specific header- should sound like an observation the way algo weaknesses does}
\nrscomment{too much info here. by the end you get to the observation--- get there faster}
Dynamic community detection is a known problem, and interestingly is also a task that applies to the co-traveler problem identified in the domain problem survey (see:~\ref{table:problem-survey})~\cite{Sawas2019, Tritsarolis2023, Tang2014, Wang2016a}.
The input graphs are either a collection of related graphs describing the same problem at $T$ time slices or may just have a stream of updates to reflect when a vertex is added, deleted or updates its position in Cartesian space.
As a result, the task is typically to identify when a community changes, or which communities persist for long periods of time (the co-traveler problem).
Many of the dynamic processing approaches batch the updates and apply a static community detection approach at each iteration then calculate the similarity in communities between each time-step, with more efficient implementations keeping the prior state in memory to reduce computation~\cite{Rossetti2018}.
These batching approaches fail in real-time settings where the batch latency is greater than the length of each timestep $T$, and provide a strong motivation for trading off accuracy and efficiency, or even tuning expectations about realistic sizes for $T$ for real-time systems.  \nrscomment{the start of that sentence was getting at what causes degradation, and then you went into tradeoffs and lost me}
