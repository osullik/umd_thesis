\subsection{Datasets Observations}

\textbf{Graph Size.}
From the survey in table~\ref{table:cd-survey} the worst-case complexity is at least quadratic in the number of vertices, particularly for the dominant algorithms like Louvain~\cite{Blondel2008} and Leiden~\cite{Traag2019}.
We can conclude that the number of vertices in the graph will be a driver of efficiency degradation in almost all cases, with many of the approaches vulnerable to dense graph structures where $|E|\gg|V|$.

\textbf{Graph Shape.}
Further, we identify that the effectiveness of community detection approaches degrades, unsurprisingly, when the communities are `fuzzy' rather than `cliquey', and when the mean degree of the graph is high.

\subsection{Implementation Observations}

\textbf{Algorithmic Weaknesses exist in Community Detection.}
Many approaches require the number of communities/clusters/partitions to be known in advance or must run an algorithm like K-Means multiple times to discover the optimal number of communities (\cite{Kernighan1970, Reichardt2004, Ng2001,White2005, Yang2016, Wang2017}), significantly increasing the computational cost when the number of communities is large. 
Knowing the number of communities a priori is not reflective of the discovery process expected in the real world~\cite{Regli2022a}.
Recursive Bisection partitioning approaches fail when there are three equally sized communities at a given level of recursion, at least one of them will be mistakenly grouped with another, and will usually be split across both. 
% \nrscomment{specifically 3?? or an amount thats not a multiple of 2?}
Greedy Agglomerative clustering approaches fail when two cliques are connected by nodes of low degree compared to the clique, pulling the `bridge' nodes into the cluster that they bridge to rather than their own. 
Most approaches are not capable of assigning a vertex to multiple communities because of the underlying assumptions about clustering and partitioning, so overlapping communities rely on graph attributes for effective community detection on heterogeneous graph structures~\cite{Reichardt2006}.
The most popular implementation (Louvain~\cite{Blondel2008}) has been shown to produce disconnected communities when the shape of the input graph contains hierarchical community structures that contain cliques joined by bridge nodes~\cite{Traag2019}.

\textbf{Modularity Optimization is not a reliable strategy.}
Modularity has several problems, despite being the dominant metric. 
Optimizing modularity is $NP$-Complete~\cite{Brandes2006}, so algorithms using modularity as the metric must take heuristic approaches to improve it. 
It is not scale-independent, that is, a scaling factor where a community with a density of internal edges of the order of the $\sqrt[]{2|E|}$ times the edges in the graph will not be resolved by modularity optimization. 
These 'loosely connected' communities are instead mistakenly combined, with the problem most prevalent when the distribution of community sizes have long tails~\cite{Fortunato2007}.

\textbf{Dynamic Processing relies on Short Batch Latency.}
% Dynamic community detection is a known problem, and interestingly is also a task that applies to the co-traveler problem identified in the domain problem survey (see:~\ref{table:problem-survey})~\cite{Sawas2019, Tritsarolis2023, Tang2014, Wang2016a}.
The inputs to dynamic graph processing algorithms are either a collection of related graphs describing the same problem at $T$ time slices or may just have a stream of updates to reflect when a vertex is added, deleted or updates its position in Cartesian space.
As a result, the task is typically to identify when a community changes, or which communities persist for long periods of time (the co-traveler problem).
Many of the dynamic processing approaches batch the updates and apply a static community detection approach at each iteration then calculate the similarity in communities between each time-step, with more efficient implementations keeping the prior state in memory to reduce computation~\cite{Rossetti2018}.
These batching approaches fail in real-time settings where the batch latency is greater than the length of each timestep $T$.

\textbf{Community Detection is suitable for Approximate and Incremental approaches.}
Clustering, Partitioning, and Community Detection are typically implemented as iterative algorithms that run until convergence but can return some information at each iteration step.
Running an approximate algorithm may provide a less accurate result in a shorter timeframe, and iterative approaches that improve as time proceeds can take longer to converge than approaches.
Datasets with hierarchical communities can have correct solutions at different resolution levels.

\textbf{Weights and Attributes are critical for segmenting fuzzy and overlapping communities.} 
Graph partitioning, clustering, and community detection are traditionally topology-only algorithms. 
However, access to some states, like the sentiment of an edge~\cite{Yang2007}, or the weights associated with edges~\cite{Rosvall2008} yield superior segments.  
The same is true of attributes, and prior knowledge about group membership of some nodes greatly improves the identification of overlapping and fuzzy communities~\cite{Zhang2013}.

Completing the exploratory analysis by proxy of a literature review reveals the bottlenecks that impact population segmentation approaches that will guide hypothesis development in the next step. 

\textbf{Efficiency Bottlenecks.} I
We expect that increasing the number of vertices and the number of edges will both degrade the efficiency of the approaches, given the worst-case complexity estimates in table~\ref{table:cd-survey}

\textbf{Effectiveness Bottlenecks.} 
The effectiveness of community detection approaches is vulnerable to fuzzy and overlapping communities, denser graphs and when `bridges' exist between clique-y structures.

\textbf{Friction Points.} 
Further friction points we identify is the requirement for apriori knowledge of the number of communities by many of the approaches, the lack of support for overlapping communities, and the lack of work in dynamic CD benchmarks.

\textbf{Curiosities and opportunities.} 
The salient opportunity is to explore the efficiency-effectiveness tradeoff by using an iterative approach to community detection that outputs interim results as they are generated and offers the opportunity to experiment with trading off efficiency and effectiveness. 
There is also an interesting constraint-based problem to find the optimal Timestep $T$ length for dynamic CD to determine how long it should wait when receiving a stream before commencing a batch update.
