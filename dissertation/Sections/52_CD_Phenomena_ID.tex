\section{Identify and Characterize Phenomena of Interest}
\label{section:52-CD-Phenomena}
We identify the phenomena of interest from the results of the problem survey (table~\ref{table:problem-survey}) that indicate a growing acceptance of trading off a perfectly effective approach that takes a long time to execute with a more efficient approach that might only provide partially correct, or low-resolution solutions. 
This section describes how we characterize the phenomena of interest as the trade-off between effectiveness, efficiency and cost. 

\subsection{Efficiency}
Measurements of efficiency do not vary much from problem to problem in \gai. 
We use the ubiquitous \textit{Execution Time} but add support for \textit{Extract, Transform, Load Time}. 
Many approaches to population segmentation use iterative algorithms, and so are well suited to providing interim solutions, making the measures of \textit{batch latency} (time from data being sent until a result it generated) and batch rate (average time to produce an interim result).

\subsection{Effectiveness}
The effectiveness of population segmentation can be evaluated in two ways -- using ground truth labels or using structural characteristics. 
Ground truth label-based evaluation is strongly preferred, and Normalized Mutual Information (NMI)~\cite{Strehl2002} is the most commonly used metric to evaluate predicted segments against ground truth. 
Scalability limitations in NMI are driving alternate methods like Network-F1 (NF1)~\cite{Rossetti2016,Rossetti2017,Rossetti2018} but have not gained widespread traction. 
Without ground truth labels, the `goodness' of a segment is calculated using a metric like \textit{modularity}~\cite{Newman2004a}, \textit{conductance}~\cite{Leskovec2010a}, or the \textit{Constant Potts Model}~\cite{Traag2011}.
These methods are limited in their ability to provide a reliable evaluation, as they effectively model the probability that the community is more connected than a random graph. 
Further, optimizing the modularity metric is $NP$-Complete~\cite{Brandes2006} and heuristic approaches perform poorly on small communities and skewed community distributions~\cite{Fortunato2007}, which are typical of scale-free graphs~\cite{Barabasi1999, Girvan2002}.

\subsection{Cost}

Cost is ideally measured as a function of power consumption or as an estimated `cost per minute' based on the specifications of the SUT. 
The \gai benchmark framework initially focuses on an Input/Output benchmark paradigm, which means the internal states of the system (power consumption, etc) are unknown.
Future work is deploying a hosted instance of the benchmark to the ARLIS \textit{Quantum Inspired Classical Computing} (QUICC) testbed, which we expect will provide measurements of power consumption by the controlling docker containers using \textit{Kepler}\footnote{{\scriptsize \url{https://sustainable-computing.io/design/architecture/}}}, and developing a daemon to deploy to a SUT to collect power consumption statistics using the \textit{PowerAPI Project}\footnote{{\scriptsize \url{https://powerapi.org/}}}, though this would be an optional component of the benchmark and limit it to running on compatible physical linux-based systems. 

As a result, cost is excluded from the Population Segmentation benchmark problem.
