\subsection{Observation Apparatus}
The observation apparatus is a component we add to our benchmark design to explicitly represent the decisions about how the benchmark will interact with the SUT.
We choose to implement an Input/Output benchmark, augmented with constraint-based hypotheses testing.
While the Input/Output approach prevents us from monitoring the internal state of the system, it is maximally accommodating of researchers altering the hardware, software stack and implementation.
System variance reflects the real-world implications of the benchmark, if someone can afford to buy and run expensive hardware to achieve better results, they are free to do so.

More specifically we choose the Input/Output model to accommodate the huge variety in \gai acceleration approaches. 
Hardware accelerator designs~\cite{Aananthakrishnan2020, Fu2014, Wang2016, Sundaram2015, Liu2019, Wu2015, Zhang2023, Dadu2021, Chen2020, Song2016, Ham2016, Dysart2016}, GNN accelerator designs~\cite{Wu2021a, Baruah2021, Zhang2023, Kiningham2022, Yan2020, Lin2023, Zhang2023b, Lin2024, Zhu2023, Zhong2023, Zhang2023a, Chen2023, Abi2023} are proliferating rapidly. 
Implementations exist for canonical sequential~\cite{Blondel2008}, distributed~\cite{Ghosh2018}, shared-memory~\cite{Ghosh2019} on CPU and GPU~\cite{Wang2016}, as well as linear-algebraic approaches for CPU~\cite{Low2020} and GPU~\cite{Mattson2019} for community detection approaches.
Configuring instrumentation for all of these systems is infeasible, and a hosted benchmark would exclude too many of the approaches, leading us to an enhanced Input/Output model for the benchmark.

\subsection{Datasets}
Datasets in the \gai population segmentation problem are expected to be large, scale-free and heterogeneous, attributed graphs.
The hypotheses identify the requirement to scale the number of vertices, the density of the graphs, the distribution of degrees, the distribution of communities and optionally include weights and attributes. 
The review of Community Detection problems also shows that community detection can be applied to dynamic graphs.

To stress across these parameters, we first look for datasets in the \textit{Tiny, Small, Medium} and \textit{Large} range. 
We define Tiny datasets as those that fit in the cache of a commercial system, $<60$Mb\footnote{\scriptsize{Based on Intel Xeon Gold \url{https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/products/details/processors/xeon/scalable/gold/products.html}}}).
Small can be processed in memory on a personal computer, no larger than $\sim16$Gb, the medium should fit in the memory of a commercial system, no larger than $\sim1$Tb and large should be bigger than what will fit in the memory of a single commercial system (at least $\sim5$Tb).

Building on the review of dataset coverage in table~\ref{table:cd-datasets}, we should aim for full coverage of scale-free, uniform-random and mesh shapes.
To enable evaluation, all datasets require ground-truth communities/clusters/partitions for NMI calculation.
% We expect most real-world datasets to be medium to large-scale-free graphs so focus effort here accordingly (see section~\ref{section:51-CD-Problem}).
To test the extra stress on memory, and provide the opportunity for approaches to leverage the information in their solutions, some datasets should be weighted and attributed, and partial assignments of vertices to communities should be available. 
There should be dynamic graphs suitable for batch updating and continuous streaming so that dynamic processing algorithms can be evaluated alongside static solutions.
Finally, to stress effectiveness, graphs should be available with varying community distributions, mean degree, `fuzziness' and overlapping and nested communities.

Our datasets are summarized in table~\ref{table:cd-datasets}.
We identify seven suitable datasets from the SNAP repository ranging from tiny to medium in size are scale-free in shape and cover the distribution of community sizes from small to large. 
Noting the lack of mesh and random graphs, we generate our own.

\subsubsection{Mesh Graph Generation}
There were no publicly available mesh graphs with ground truth community labels. 
We develop a tool to generate directed, weighted, attributed graphs with hierarchical and overlapping communities from OpenStreetMap (OSM) road network data\footnote{\scriptsize Tool available at \url{https://github.com/osullik/umd_thesis/blob/main/md_roads/osmg.py}}. 
Our tool queries the OSM API for a given city, county or state, extracts the road segments (ways) and creates a vertex where the ends of two segments meet or where two segments intersect. 
We construct the graph using Python's NetworkX~\cite{Hagberg2008} and assign each vertex the attributes of its latitude, longitude and the name of the street it comes from. 
We group the vertices by subordinate communities. 
For example, a state has community groups at the county and city levels.
Where a road crosses a border, it is assigned to a special group that is a member of both communities. 
We use the tool to create six tiny and small datasets with very low density, wide diameters, and a broad distribution of community counts across multiple resolutions. 

\subsubsection{Random Graph Generation}

While random graphs are not an effective substitute for real-world graphs from the target domain, they are a useful supplemental tool to cover gaps in real-world data and to develop datasets to stress specific system components.
We lack coverage of the spectrum of graph shapes between mesh and scale-free.  
Imitative generators are not yet at the maturity to generate the medium and large datasets we require, nor are suitable statistical summaries of graphs available to seed imitative generators.
Specific community-detection generators like LFR~\cite{Lancichinetti2008}, AcMARK~\cite{Maekawa2019}, GenCAT~\cite{Maekawa2023} can generate graphs with ground-truth communities following mathematical distributions.

\subsection{Limitations}
% \nrscomment{theres a lot here- i think it is worth discussing some of the potential failure cases of your design, but do it more concisely, especially cutting down the last part about caching to a sentence or two}
Several limitations exist in the current design of the population segmentation problem benchmark. 
First, transferring the data is highly sensitive to network bandwidth and latency, disadvantaging remote researchers. 
Using static datasets can lead to gaming the system, which is why we implement the randomized problem parameters, hash checks and network data summaries to verify that the benchmark process is being followed. 
It is still possible to game it, but it requires a more deliberate effort.
