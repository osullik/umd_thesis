\section{Construct the Investigation Apparatus}
\label{section:55-CD-Apparatus}

The apparatus is the physical instantiation of the problem benchmark.
Figure~\ref{Figure:ch1-framework} shows how the apparatus is derived from each process step. 
Three of the components (datasets, tasks, and metrics) are consistent with traditional benchmarking approaches (see Figure~\ref{Figure:benchmark-components}).
We add the explicit definition of the observation apparatus. 
These four components are the concrete implementation of the benchmark. 
Combined, they have two key roles. 
First, they must interact with the \ac{SUT} -- problems and datasets go in, and solutions for evaluation come out.
Second, they must monitor the phenomena emerging from the \ac{SUT} throughout the process.
Figure~\ref{Figure:cd-apparatus} is the envisaged workflow for the population segmentation problem benchmark, showing how the physical components of the observation apparatus interact between the benchmark and the \ac{SUT}.

The population segmentation benchmark workflow is representative of the expected workflow for most problems in the \gai taxonomy because, as a \ac{GM} task, it is in the family of \gai problems used most by the \ac{IandS} community.
It can be solved in multiple ways (including with \ac{GNN}s), focuses on graph topology but can also use attribute information, admits approximate and iterative solutions, has a linear algebraic implementation, and has real-world temporal constraints that may benefit from trading effectiveness for efficiency.
In the remainder of this section, we summarize the concrete components of the apparatus, showing how they were created and identifying expansion opportunities. 

\subsection{Observation Apparatus}
The observation apparatus is a component we include in our benchmark design to explicitly represent the decisions about how the benchmark will interact with the \ac{SUT}.
We implement an Input/Output benchmark, augmented with constraint-based hypotheses testing.
While the Input/Output approach prevents us from monitoring the system's internal state, it maximally accommodates researchers altering the hardware, software stack, and implementation.
System variance reflects the benchmark's real-world implications; if someone can afford to buy and run expensive hardware to achieve better results, they are free to do so.

More specifically, we choose the Input/Output model to accommodate the wide variety in \gai acceleration approaches.
For hardware-based acceleration, \ac{GHA}~\cite{Aananthakrishnan2020, Fu2014, Wang2016, Sundaram2015, Liu2019, Wu2015, Zhang2023, Dadu2021, Chen2020, Song2016, Ham2016, Dysart2016} and \ac{GNN} accelerators~\cite{Wu2021a, Baruah2021, Zhang2023, Kiningham2022, Yan2020, Lin2023, Zhang2023b, Lin2024, Zhu2023, Zhong2023, Zhang2023a, Chen2023, Abi2023} are proliferating rapidly, and a future-proof benchmarking framework must accommodate as many variations as possible to remain relevant.
% 
Represenation-based acceleration techniques for \ac{CD} have created multiple different implementations including canonical sequential~\cite{Blondel2008}, distributed~\cite{Ghosh2018}, shared-memory~\cite{Ghosh2019} on \ac{CPU} and \ac{GPU}~\cite{Wang2016}, as well as linear-algebraic approaches for \ac{CPU}~\cite{Low2020} and \ac{GPU}~\cite{Mattson2019}.
Configuring instrumentation for all of these systems is infeasible, and a hosted benchmark would exclude too many of the approaches, leading us to select an enhanced Input/Output model for the benchmark. 

\subsection{Datasets}
Datasets in the \gai population segmentation problem are expected to be large, scale-free, and heterogeneous attributed graphs.
The hypotheses identify the requirement to scale the number of vertices, the density of the graphs, the distribution of degrees, and the distribution of communities, and they optionally include weights and attributes. 
The review of \ac{CD} problems also shows that \ac{CD} can be applied to dynamic graphs.

To stress across these parameters, we first look for datasets in the \textit{Tiny, Small, Medium, Large} and \textit{Huge} range. 
We define \textit{Tiny} datasets as those that fit in the cache of a commercial system, $<60$Mb\footnote{\scriptsize{Based on Intel Xeon Gold (\url{https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/products/details/processors/xeon/scalable/gold/products.html}})}.
\textit{Small} can be processed in memory on a personal computer, no larger than $\sim16$Gb, the \textit{medium} should fit in the memory of a commercial system, no larger than $\sim1$Tb, \textit{large} should be fit within the memory of a commercial system (smaller than $5$Tb), and \textit{Huge} should be more extensive than what will fit in the memory of a single commercial system (at least $\sim5$Tb).

Building on the review of dataset coverage in  Table~\ref{Table:cd-datasets}, we should aim for full coverage of scale-free, uniform-random, and mesh shapes.
All datasets require ground-truth communities/clusters/partitions for \ac{NMI} calculation.
Some datasets should be weighted and attributed, and partial assignments of vertices to communities should be made available as an additional stress on memory and to allow approaches to exploit this additional information in their solutions. 
Dynamic graphs should be suitable for batch updating and continuous streaming to evaluate dynamic processing algorithms alongside static solutions.
Finally, graphs should be available to stress effectiveness with varying community distributions, mean degree, `fuzziness,' and overlapping and nested communities.

Our datasets are summarized in Table~\ref{Table:cd-datasets}, with coverage of our target efficiency and effectiveness difficulty levels shown in Figure~\ref{Figure:cd-datasets}.
We identify seven suitable datasets from the \ac{SLNDC} repository ranging from tiny to medium, which are scale-free and cover the distribution of community sizes from small to large. 
Noting the lack of mesh and random graphs, we generate our own.

\subsubsection{Mesh Graph Generation}
There were no publicly available mesh graphs with ground truth community labels. 
We develop a tool to generate directed, weighted, attributed graphs with hierarchical and overlapping communities from \ac{OSM} road network data\footnote{\scriptsize tool available at \url{https://github.com/osullik/umd_thesis/blob/main/md_roads/osmg.py}}. 
Our tool queries the \ac{OSM} \ac{API} for a given city, county, or state, extracts the road segments (ways), and creates a vertex where segment ends meet or multiple segments intersect. 
We construct the graph using Python's NetworkX~\cite{Hagberg2008} and assign each vertex the attributes of its latitude, longitude, and street name. 
We group the vertices by subordinate communities. 
For example, a state has community groups at the county and city levels.
Where a road crosses a border, it is assigned to a particular group as a member of both communities. 
We use the tool to create six tiny and small datasets with very low density, wide diameters, and a broad distribution of community counts across multiple resolutions. 

\subsubsection{Random Graph Generation}

While random graphs are not an adequate substitute for real-world graphs from the target domain, they are helpful supplemental tools for covering real-world data gaps and developing datasets to stress specific system components.
As Figure~\ref{Figure:cd-datasets} shows, we lack real-world coverage of the spectrum of graph shapes between mesh and scale-free. 
Imitative generators are not yet mature enough to generate the medium and large datasets we require, and suitable statistical summaries of graphs are not available to seed imitative generators.
Specific community-detection generators like LFR~\cite{Lancichinetti2008}, AcMARK~\cite{Maekawa2019}, and GenCAT~\cite{Maekawa2023} can generate graphs with ground-truth communities following mathematical distributions, and we use them to fill in the gaps shown in Figure~\ref{Figure:cd-datasets}.

\subsection{Tasks}
We draw on the problems identified in Table~\ref{Table:cd-problem-definition} for our specific tasks. 
To enhance the benchmark, we intend for the tasks to be publicly and generically specified.
That means the complete list of problem-centric task templates will be available to benchmark users in advance to prepare and test systems. 
However, the specific values will only be provided at experiment time to ensure fairness.
For example, the public generic definition might be \texttt{`What other nodes are in the same group as vertex (x)?'}, but when the benchmark is actually run, the specific problem of \texttt{``What other nodes are in the same group as the vertex with name==`Mr\_Black' ''?}
Figure~\ref{Figure:cd-apparatus} shows the back-and-forth process between the SUT and the benchmark as steps $1-4$, which is how all tasks are provided to the \ac{SUT} by the \ac{BS}.

\subsection{Limitations}
Several limitations exist in the current design of the population segmentation problem benchmark. 
First, data transfer is highly sensitive to network bandwidth and latency, disadvantaging remote systems. 
Given that the benchmark uses static datasets, we minimize the chances that systems can `cheat' by implementing randomized problem parameters, hash checks, and graph data summaries to verify that the benchmark process is being followed.
