\section{Graph Definitions}
\label{section:21-graph-definitions}

Graphs are the fundamental data structure common to \gai problems. Below we define the components of a graph and discuss some of the core properties a graph can possess.

\define{Graph ($G$)}{The data structure comprising of the related vertices and edges such that $G=\{V,E\}$. Also called a `network'. $|G|$ represents the size of the graph, and when used in this general sense it means the sum of the number of vertices and edges.}
\define{Vertex ($V$)}{Represents an `entity' or `thing'. Also called a node. $|V|$ is the number of vertices (size of the vertex set).}
\define{Edge ($E$)}{Represents a relationship between two vertices. Also called arcs or links. $|E|$  represents the number of edges (size of the edge set).}
\define{Attribute}{A label, property or other information that is encoded in a vertex or edge of a graph. Graphs with attributes are called attributed graphs.}
\define{Weight}{A numerical value assigned to an edge to describe some real-world value it represents, like the number of citations, length of a road, or cost of an action. Graphs with weights are called weighted graphs.}
\define{Direction}{The flow of information across an edge from the source node to the destination node. Graphs having edges with direction are called directed graphs.}
\define{Multi-Edge}{A special case of directed edge where more than one edge may exist between a given pair of vertices. Graphs with multi-edges are called multi-graphs and are typical of heterogeneous graphs like knowledge graphs.} 
\define{Hyper-Edge}{An edge that connects an arbitrary number of vertices. Graphs with hyper-edges are called hyper-graphs.}\\


\noindent Graphs have the following characteristics that define their size and shape, including their topology, degree distribution and diameter. 

\define{Topology}{The structural characteristics of a graph that emerge when edges link vertices together, regardless of attributes or edge weights. Common topologies include mesh, scale-free and uniform-random.}
\define{Degree}{The count of edges at a given vertex. Frequently separated into degree$_{out}$ and degree$_{in}$ in directed graphs.}
\define{Average Degree}{The mean degree across all vertices in the graph. A higher mean degree indicates a denser graph.}
\define{Degree Distribution}{The distribution of vertex degrees in a given graph. Can follow any statistical distribution and correlates strongly with known graph processing bottlenecks.}
\define{Neighborhood}{The collection of vertices that have edges directly connecting them to a given vertex. The $K$-hop neighborhood is the collection of vertices within $K$ edges of a given vertex for example a 'friend of a friend'.}
\define{Diameter}{The length of the shortest path between the two most distant vertices in a graph, where distance is measured by the number of hops between vertices. Sparser graphs tend to have larger diameters because there are fewer high-degree nodes to bridge gaps between otherwise distant nodes.}\\

%The following definitions describe the characteristics of graphs: 
\noindent Although graphs can have a variety of characteristics, they can be broadly categorized as simple or heterogeneous, and static or dynamic.

\define{Simple Graph}{The most basic form of a graph. It may be directed, weighted, and attributed, but the attributes are of a consistent type (e.g. all vertex labels are of type 'name').}
\define{Heterogeneous Graph}{A graph with multiple `types' of vertex, such as a social graph that has vertices to represent `person' and vertices to represent `organization'. Heterogeneous graphs are more complicated to process than simple graphs because they require type-checking before acting on a given vertex or edge.}
\define{Static Graph}{A graph whose vertices, edges, weights, and attributes are known apriori and do not change. Static graphs are generally simpler to process than dynamic graphs.}
\define{Dynamic Graph}{A graph that evolves with its vertices, edges, attributes, and weights being created, updated, and deleted over time. Also called a streaming graph. Dynamic graphs introduce temporal constraints on graph processing that make many of the well-researched static approaches unviable.}
