\section{Motivation}
Graphs are expressive data structures that encode evidence as vertices and contextual relationships between evidence as edges. 
Graphs lend themselves well to learning and reasoning tasks as powerful abstractions of the real world, encompassing an emerging area of work we term Graph-Based Artificial Intelligence (\gai).
We taxonomize \gai within the existing AI landscape, covering the well-known areas of \ac{GP}~\cite{Smith2021, Zhou2020a, Xia2021} and \ac{GM}~\cite{Chen2022a} and extending those to include \ac{GPS}, \ac{GR}, \ac{GA}, and \ac{GT}, which are detailed in Figure~\ref{figure:gai-taxonomy}.
Across these areas, \gai includes tasks like structural analysis, inference over graphs, and applications of graphs to AI problems like model transparency, explainability, and interpretability.

\input{Figures/Diagrams/13_gai_taxonomy}

Although graphs are expressive and broadly applicable to various AI problems, they impose a significant computational burden on systems that operate \gai.
\gai algorithms that operate on graphs frequently have quadratic or worse complexity, which limits their usability on real-world graphs containing billions or trillions of vertices. 
Modern general-purpose processors use caching and prefetching to speed up computation over large non-graph inputs.
The challenge with graphs is that most \gai algorithms exploit the graph's structure in processing, which requires access to the data in topological order.
That requirement conflicts with graph storage, which is typically chronological in the order that vertices are created, meaning topologically close vertices are not necessarily proximal in memory. 
Without caching benefits, \gai algorithms rely on data retrieved from main memory, which causes memory latency and bandwidth bottlenecks that limit scalability and prevent \gai from being deployed on real-world problems that might otherwise benefit from it. 

\section{Problem and Background}
\label{section:11-introduction-to-problem}

Common approaches to speeding up \gai processing typically use hardware, like specialized accelerators or distributed processing; representation, like custom data structures or abstraction models; or algorithms, like parallel or approximate methods.
These approaches are interdependent, meaning decisions about optimizing hardware can limit choices about parallelism or programming models, which makes it difficult to determine the optimal configuration for a given collection of problems. 

This thesis aims to determine \textit{if}, \textit{how}, and \textit{when} to speed up problem-solving on \gai given a set of operating \textit{constraints}.
Specifically, we consider how to effectively combine representations, hardware accelerators, and algorithms to speed up \gai and under what conditions a specific combination is appropriate for a use case given some constraints on a solution's efficiency, effectiveness, and cost.
Prior approaches to identifying if, how, and when to accelerate a \gai system rely on generic competitive benchmarks or invasive workload analysis, both of which provide incomplete views of a \gai system's performance. 

\textit{Benchmarking} is a process by which systems or components are compared according to some criteria, which is used to determine their relative performance~\cite{Kistowski2015}.
Most benchmarks for \gai take a `black-box' approach to competing systems based on \textit{efficiency} or \textit{effectiveness} metrics but rarely both, and never their interactions.
Benchmarks typically focus on evaluating isolated \textit{kernel} level tasks rather than more complicated real-world problems and typically use a small selection of datasets that do not cover the breadth of expected input sizes and shapes.
Existing benchmarks also lack support for dynamic graph processing approaches, especially comparing dynamic and static approaches under the same evaluation framework.

Alternatively, \textit{workload analyses} are `white-box' approaches that analyze the internal state of a system while completing a workload to identify bottlenecks. 
Although workload analyses yield detailed information about how to accelerate the system under test, they require access to the system for profiling and access to the code for instrumentation. 
The invasive configuration requirements mean workload analyses cannot be scaled for general-purpose comparisons, making them unsuitable for broad application to \gai.

In addition to the choice in the analytic method, several other challenges underlay the broader problem of deciding if, how, and when to improve \gai under some constraints, which we outline in Figure~\ref{figure:gai-challenges}.
Taking a user's top-down perspective of the challenge hierarchy, the user must first decide the degree to which effectiveness should be traded for efficiency or cost.
The need to make this trade-off stems from the real-world constraints imposed on \gai systems, such as a requirement to generate a recurring solution at a pace more frequent than the algorithm's runtime allows.
Further, most graph hardware accelerators are specialized \ac{ASIC} and are optimized to speed up a specific algorithm, representation, abstraction, and programming model.
The problem with \ac{ASIC}s is that a given \gai system may need to support many graph tasks, so a specialized accelerator may not garner a broad enough improvement to justify its cost investment. 
These real-world challenges and trade-offs are particularly pronounced for \gai because of the inherent nature of graph processing that underlies \gai problems.
Graphs of different shapes and sizes unpredictably influence algorithmic complexity, including exacerbating irregular memory accesses and computation patterns that undermine spatial and temporal locality. 
When combined with the data dependence, the lack of standard graph representation methods, abstraction models, or a set of primitive operations further prevents a unity of effort toward accelerating graph processing across all graph tasks.

\section{Developing the \gai benchmark framework and \gai benchmark methodology.}
\label{section:graph-based-ai-problem-methodology}

\input{Figures/Diagrams/23_grey_box_benchmark}

\input{Figures/Diagrams/04_benchmark_framework}

We address these \gai issues by contributing our \gai benchmark framework, shown in figure~\ref{figure:gai-framework-overview}. 
It is an Input/Output benchmark that evaluates the performance of a \ac{SUT} using an Input/Output observation apparatus to provide the \ac{SUT} problem-centric tasks and data, record the time to solution, and verify the correctness of an answer. 
Each \gai benchmark in the \gai benchmark framework is independent, and we contribute an empirical methodology for constructing a problem-centric, accelerator-agnostic `grey-box' \gai benchmark that can evaluate both the \textit{efficiency} and the \textit{effectiveness} of dynamic and static \gai implementations and systems.

By `grey-box', we mean an approach to benchmarking between the fully opaque black-box view and the invasive white-box view (Figure~\ref{figure:grey-box}). 
In implementing a grey-box view, we use the \gai benchmark design methodology to identify expected system and implementation bottlenecks and design adversarial inputs we expect to stimulate those bottlenecks. 
By evaluating the output and analyzing the efficiency and effectiveness across datasets, we can infer which expected bottleneck will likely cause an observed output without invasive instrumentation.

We employ a formal method (Figure~\ref{figure:ch1-framework}) that emphasizes the transparency, verifiability, and reproducibility of benchmark problems and balances the generality of competitive benchmarks and the detail of workload analyses without requiring invasive system access. 
Our method comprises six steps that generate the components of the benchmark specification and the observation apparatus.

The process begins by identifying a use case to ground the problem in real-world datasets and current approaches. 
A more detailed analysis of the problem yields information about the size and shape of the data and formally specifies the everyday tasks associated with that problem in natural language. 
Formalizing the study's phenomena includes identifying what specific interactions of dataset and task are likely to yield interesting results, providing the first significant contribution to the problem's `grey box' approach. 
Considering the phenomena, exploratory analysis of the domain builds the base of knowledge to make initial hypotheses about expected behavior under some conditions. 
The hypotheses drive the data collection to study the phenomena of interest and observed deviation from those hypotheses indicates interesting behavior warranting further study. 
The construction of the apparatus involves collecting and generating the datasets that will be used to trigger the conditions the hypotheses predict will stimulate the phenomena of interest, which may require developing \ac{API} extensions to facilitate the problem and evaluate the solutions. 

Our proposed methodology for designing benchmark problems yields a collection of datasets, tasks, and metrics that combine to provide a `grey box' view of the \ac{SUT}, stimulating phenomena of interest according to the defined hypotheses. 
The repeatable process we propose delivers an extensible framework that can be continually improved to evaluate new \gai problems as they emerge.


\section{Applying our \gai benchmark framework}

Following our proposed methodology, we construct a \gai benchmark for the Population Segmentation use case in the \ac{IandS} domain.

% The benchmark definition of our \gai framework comprises independent benchmark problem modules containing a collection of datasets and problems selected to stress the efficiency and effectiveness of the \ac{SUT}. 

%Tasks
Following our \textit{problem-centric} methodology, the population segmentation tasks we select for our benchmark are real-world problem statements rather than simplified kernels. 
For example, we include the task `\textit{What are the population segments, and who is in each?}' instead of the common \textit{kernel} task `\textit{run \ac{GC}}'.

%Datsets
To achieve our `grey-box' effect, we identify that the primary contributor to degraded \textit{efficiency} is increasing dataset size and that the primary contributor to degraded benchmark \textit{effectiveness} is increasing `fuzziness' in community structure. 
We select real-world scale-free datasets with ground-truth community labels from the \ac{SLNDC} dataset and generate real-world mesh datasets using a tool we create for converting \ac{OSM} road network data to directed, weighted, attributed graphs with ground-truth communities.
To address the gaps in real-world data for datasets of large size and middling `fuzziness' we use the \ac{LFR}~\cite{Lancichinetti2008} and \textit{GenCAT}~\cite{Maekawa2023} community data generators. 

%Metrics
We select the effectiveness metric of \ac{NMI} to measure the similarity of predicted communities to ground truth and the efficiency metrics of \ac{ExT} and \ac{BL}, allowing us to compare static and dynamic approaches as well as the trade-off between efficiency and effectiveness all under the same framework.

\section{Applications  and impact of the \gai benchmarking framework}
Our \gai benchmark framework is a flexible experimentation and exploratory analysis tool for analysts, engineers, and researchers.
Analysts who routinely work with graph data and apply \gai to solve problems can use the \gai benchmark to understand better the sizes and shapes of inputs that will impact their work performance and use prior results to build estimates for \gai execution time. 
Engineers can use the \gai benchmark to evaluate which systems they should procure and to make architectural decisions about how their system implements \gai to maximize the efficiency and effectiveness across the breadth of tasks the system must support.
Researchers building new \gai algorithms can use the \gai benchmark framework to experimentally vary the hardware and implementation details to find bottlenecks as a function of input datasets and problems. 
Because the benchmark specification is independent of the \ac{SUT}, an analyst, engineer, or researcher can iterate quickly, modifying their hardware, software stack, and implementation while quickly measuring the corresponding performance changes that inform further work.
