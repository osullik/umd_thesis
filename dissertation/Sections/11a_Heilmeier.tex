\section{Introduction to the Problem}
\label{section:11-introduction-to-problem}
Our \gai Benchmarking Framework provides a mechanism to determine \textit{if}, \textit{how}, and \textit{when} to speed up problem-solving on linked data given a set of operating constraints. 
It builds on prior work in benchmarking and workload analyses to create a balanced benchmark that is problem-centric and accelerator-agnostic and evaluates both the \textit{efficiency} and \textit{effectiveness} of a system under test, bringing immediate benefit to analysts, engineers, and researchers. 

\subsection{Problem Definition}
This thesis aims to determine \textit{if}, \textit{how}, and \textit{when} to speed up problem-solving on linked data given a set of operating \textit{constraints}.
\textit{How} refers to effectively combining the representations, hardware accelerators, and algorithms to speed up \gai, while \textit{if} and \textit{when} answer when a specific combination is appropriate for the use case. 
In the real world, \textit{constraints} on a solution's efficiency, effectiveness, and cost can limit a user's freedom in designing a system, directly impacting the if, how and when dimensions of the problem, and so are explicitly considered.

\subsection{Prior Work}
Prior approaches to identifying if, how, and when to accelerate a \gai system do not balance a competitive benchmark's general utility and scalability with the insight produced by an invasive workload analysis. 

\textit{Benchmarking} is a process by which systems or components are compared according to some criteria, which is used to determine their relative performance~\cite{Kistowski2015}.
Most benchmarks for \gai take a `black-box' approach to competing systems based on metrics of \textit{efficiency} or \textit{effectiveness} but rarely both, and never their interactions.
They have typically focused on evaluating isolated \textit{kernel} level tasks rather than more complicated problems consistent with the real world. 
They typically use a small selection of datasets that do not necessarily represent the real world and do not cover the breadth of expected input sizes and shapes. 
More support is needed for dynamic graph processing, and work has yet to be done comparing dynamic and static approaches under the same evaluation framework. 

Alternatively, workload analyses are `white-box' approaches that analyze the internal state of a system while completing a workload to identify bottlenecks. 
While they yield detailed information, they require access to the system under test for profiling and access to the code to add instrumentation. 
The invasive configuration requirements mean workload analyses cannot be scaled for general-purpose comparisons. Therefore, their use is unsuitable for achieving the broad coverage the \gai benchmark framework pursues.

\subsection{The \gai Benchmark Framework}
We introduce the \gai Benchmark Framework as a solution that balances the generality of competitive benchmarks and the detail of workload analyses without requiring invasive system access. 
We leverage constraint theory with an empirical approach to algorithmic analysis to develop inputs that specifically trigger bottleneck behavior. 
We then use our observations of those behaviors to develop a `grey-box' understanding of the system's internal state. 
Our approach is \textit{problem-centric}, meaning that the benchmark tasks are real-world problem statements rather than simplified kernels. 
For example, the \textit{kernel} task `\textit{run \ac{BFS}}' could be presented in problem-centric form as `\textit{Find the neighbors of vertex X}.'
The problem-centric view better captures the deployment of a system in the real world and encourages innovative approaches by not constraining the benchmark to specific approaches. 

The framework adopts an Input/Output that maximizes the degree of freedom of a benchmark user. 
Users can vary their hardware, software stack, and implementation without changing how their code interfaces with the benchmark. 
This allows for easy experimentation with different combinations of acceleration techniques to determine what works best for their use case. 
Finally, our evaluation approach explores the interaction between efficiency and effectiveness, recognizing that a fast partial solution can be more beneficial than a slow complete one.
Our more nuanced view of effectiveness makes it possible to evaluate dynamic and static approaches using the same framework.

\subsection{Impact for Target User Groups}
The \gai Benchmark Framework is a flexible tool for experimentation and exploratory analysis for analysts, engineers, and researchers.
Analysts who routinely work with graph data and apply \gai to solve problems can use the \gai benchmark to understand better the sizes and shapes of inputs that will impact their work performance and use prior results to build estimates for \gai execution time. 
Engineers can use the \gai benchmark to evaluate which systems they should procure and to make architectural decisions about how their system implements \gai to maximize the efficiency and effectiveness across the breadth of tasks the system must support.
Researchers building \gai can use the \gai Benchmark Framework to experimentally vary the hardware and implementation details to find the bottlenecks in \gai as a function of the input datasets and real-world problems it solves. 
The ability to satisfy the needs of these three distinct user groups is a valuable feature of the \gai benchmark framework.
