\section{Graph Classes}
\label{section:21-graph-classes}

The size and topology directly impact the effectiveness and efficiency of graph processing. 
While the structure of graphs exists on a spectrum and is influenced by several factors, it is useful to identify key categories and their characteristics. 
\textit{Random, Scale-Free} and \textit{Mesh} are commonly identified groupings, with \textit{non-native} graphs emerging as another category.

A \textit{Uniform Random Graph} is the typical baseline for evaluating graph algorithms. 
A random graph is synthetic, and heavily limited in evaluating the effectiveness of algorithms in `real world' scenarios because random graphs do not occur in the real world. 
It is generated by creating a set of vertices and then creating an edge between each vertex in the set with a given probability. 
One of the most well-known random graph generation models is the Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi model~\cite{Erdhos1960}.

\define{Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi Random Graph}{given $G(n,p)$ where $n$ is the number of vertices to create and $p$ is the probability of any two vertices being connected by an edge, the generator creates $n$ vertices $\{v \in V\}$, and the set of edges $E = \{(u,v)|u,v \in V, u \neq v$ with probability $P$\} yielding the random graph $G=\{V, E\}$.}\\

\noindent Two other random graph generation methodologies permeate widely. 
Both the \textit{Random Matrix} (RMAT)~\cite{Chakrabarti2004} and \textit{Kronecker}~\cite{Leskovec2010} methodologies aim to produce \textit{Skewed Random Graphs}, and have been used in the Graph500 benchmark. 

\define{RMAT Random Graph}{Given $G(n,a,b,c,d)$ $2^{n}$ number of vertices to create, and $a,b,c$ and $d$ are a distribution of probabilities that add to $1$. The RMAT generation process recursively subdivides the adjacency matrix of a graph into quadrants and creates an edge in each quadrant with probabilities $a,b,c$ and $d$}.

\define{Kronecker Random Graph}{Given a seed graph $G$ with size $n$ and a defined number of iterations $i$, the genration process recursively takes the Kronecker product of $G$ with itself, increasing the size of the graph by $n^{2}$ each iteration.}\\

\noindent the RMAT and Kronecker graphs simulate graphs occurring in the real world that exhibit a skewed degree distribution. 
Specifically, they aim to generate graphs in a \textit{Scale Free} distribution. 

\define{Scale-Free Graph}{Graphs where the vertex degrees follow a power-law distribution. Common in social networks, they form because the number of vertices tends to increase over time, and new vertices preferentially attach to vertices that already have high degrees~\cite{Barabasi1999a}. They tend to have a small diameter (the `small world' property) and a large variance in neighborhood size, and a `global-neighborhood' characteristic.}\\

Scale-free graphs have been shown to improve locality in graph processing~\cite{Beamer2015}, but are more difficult for parallel algorithms to process because they are difficult to cleanly partition and the variance in neighborhood size leads to imbalanced workload between worker nodes.

The opposite end of the spectrum from scale-free graphs are \textit{Mesh} graphs. 

\define{Mesh Graph}{A graph that is characterized by a regular degree distribution, generally low mean degree and large diameter. Mesh graphs are often derived from physical phenomena like road networks and have a more `local-neighborhood' characteristic and wide diameter.}\\

Mesh Graphs are typically better suited to parallel processing because their large diameter and local neighborhood structure mean there are minimal cross-partition dependencies, and the workload is more balanced. 
However, for algorithms that must visit the entire graph each iteration (like PageRank), the poor spatial locality of the vertices in memory incurs additional processing latency. 
The expressive power of graphs is being explored beyond `graph-native' problems. 

\define{Graph-Native Problem}{A problem that is intrinsically structured like a graph. For example, a social media friends graph.}

\define{Non-Native Graph Problem}{A problem that has been transformed to a graph problem for processing. For example, aggregating pixels by color to vertices and drawing edges between the vertices to represent an image in lower dimensional space~\cite{Cen2023}.}\\

Non-native graph formats are introducing new graph shapes that will likely have further unpredictable downstream impacts on the effectiveness and efficiency of graph processing.
