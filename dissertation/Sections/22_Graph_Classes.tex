\section{Graph Classes}
\label{section:21-graph-classes}

Graphs can be constructed synthetically, or they can come from real data.
The size and topology directly impact the effectiveness and efficiency of graph processing. 
While the structure of graphs exists on a spectrum and is influenced by several factors, it is useful to identify key categories and their characteristics. 
\textit{Random, Scale-Free} and \textit{Mesh} are commonly identified groupings, with \textit{non-native} graphs emerging as another category.

\subsection{Random Graphs}
A random graph is synthetic. 
It is generated by creating a set of vertices and then creating an edge between each vertex in the set with a given probability. 
To improve the `realism' of the graph, the probabilities can be derived from some pre-existing distribution.
Despite its limited applicability in the real world, a \textit{Uniform Random Graph} is the typical baseline for evaluating graph algorithms. 
One of the most well-known random graph generation models is the Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi model~\cite{Erdhos1960}.

\define{Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi Random Graph}{given $G(n,p)$ where $n$ is the number of vertices to create and $p$ is the probability of any two vertices being connected by an edge, the generator creates $n$ vertices $\{v \in V\}$, and the set of edges $E = \{(u,v)|u,v \in V, u \neq v$ with probability $P$\} yielding the random graph $G=\{V, E\}$.}\\

Two other random graph generation methodologies permeate widely. 
Both the \textit{Random Matrix} (RMAT)~\cite{Chakrabarti2004} and \textit{Kronecker}~\cite{Leskovec2010} methodologies aim to produce \textit{Skewed Random Graphs}, and have been used in the Graph500 benchmark. 
An \textit{RMAT Random Graph} uses four probability parameters $a,b,c$ and $d$ that add to $1.0$. 
The RMAT generation process recursively subdivides an adjacency matrix into quadrants and creates an edge in each quadrant with probabilities $a,b,c$ and $d$ until there are approximately $2^{n}$ vertices in the graph, where $n$ is a scaling factor. 
The \textit{Kronecker Random Graph} begins with a seed graph $G$, of size $n$ and a defined number of iterations $i$, the generation process recursively takes the Kronecker product of $G$ with itself, increasing the size of the graph by $n^{2}$ for $i$ iterations.

While the RMAT and Kronecker generators simulate graphs with a skewed degree distribution, they, along with the Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi graphs are limited in their utility for evaluating the effectiveness of algorithms in `real-world' scenarios.
They give an `average case' or an `expected case' for the runtime, but not necessarily a `typical case'.
Graph processing is so dependent on the topology of the input that unless the test graphs are drawn from the same distribution as the graphs that will be encountered in the real world, the results are of limited value.

\subsection{Scale-Free Graphs}

\define{Scale-Free Graph}{Graphs where the vertex degrees follow a power-law distribution. Common in social networks, they form because the number of vertices tends to increase over time, and new vertices preferentially attach to vertices that already have high degrees~\cite{Barabasi1999a}. They tend to have a small diameter (the `small world' property) a large variance in neighborhood size, and a `global-neighborhood' characteristic.}\\

\subsection{Mesh Graphs}
The opposite end of the spectrum from scale-free graphs are \textit{Mesh} graphs. 

\define{Mesh Graph}{A graph that is characterized by a regular degree distribution, generally low mean degree and large diameter. Mesh graphs are often derived from physical phenomena like road networks and have a more `local-neighborhood' characteristic and wide diameter.}\\

\textit{Graph-Native Problems} are problems that have an intrinsic graph structure. For example, a social media friends graph.
\textit{Non-Native Graph Problems} are problems that have been transformed into graph problems for processing. 
For example, aggregating pixels by color to vertices and drawing edges between the vertices to represent an image in lower dimensional space~\cite{Cen2023}.
Non-native graph formats are introducing new graph shapes that will likely have further unpredictable downstream impacts on the effectiveness and efficiency of graph processing.
