A \textit{Uniform Random Graph} is the typical baseline for evaluating graph algorithms. 
A random graph is synthetic, and heavily limited in evaluating the effectiveness of algorithms in `real world' scenarios because random graphs do not occur in the real world. 
It is generated by creating a set of vertices and then creating an edge between each vertex in the set with a given probability. 
One of the most well-known random graph generation models is the Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi model~\cite{Erdhos1960}.

\noindent the RMAT and Kronecker graphs simulate graphs occurring in the real world that exhibit a skewed degree distribution. 
Specifically, they aim to generate graphs in a \textit{Scale Free} distribution. 

\define{Scale-Free Graph}{Graphs where the vertex degrees follow a power-law distribution. Common in social networks, they form because the number of vertices tends to increase over time, and new vertices preferentially attach to vertices that already have high degrees~\cite{Barabasi1999a}. They tend to have a small diameter (the `small world' property) and a large variance in neighborhood size, and a `global-neighborhood' characteristic.}\\

The opposite end of the spectrum from scale-free graphs are \textit{Mesh} graphs. 

\define{Mesh Graph}{A graph that is characterized by a regular degree distribution, generally low mean degree and large diameter. Mesh graphs are often derived from physical phenomena like road networks and have a more `local-neighborhood' characteristic and wide diameter.}\\

Mesh Graphs are typically better suited to parallel processing because their large diameter and local neighborhood structure mean there are minimal cross-partition dependencies, and the workload is more balanced. 
However, for algorithms that must visit the entire graph each iteration (like PageRank), the poor spatial locality of the vertices in memory incurs additional processing latency. 
The expressive power of graphs is being explored beyond `graph-native' problems.
