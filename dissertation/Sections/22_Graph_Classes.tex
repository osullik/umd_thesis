\section{Graph Classes}
\label{section:22-graph-classes}

Graphs can be constructed synthetically, or they can come from real data.
The size and topology directly impact the effectiveness and efficiency of graph processing. 
While the structure of graphs exists on a spectrum and is influenced by several factors, it is useful to identify key categories and their characteristics. 
\textit{Scale-Free, Mesh} and \textit{Random} are commonly identified groupings, with \textit{non-native} graphs emerging as another category.

\subsection{Scale-Free Graphs}

Scale-free graphs are a naturally occurring graph class characterized by a distribution of vertex degrees following a power-law distribution. 
Common in social networks, they form because the number of vertices tends to increase over time, and new vertices preferentially attach to vertices that already have high degrees~\cite{Barabasi1999a}. 
They tend to have a small diameter, a large variance in neighborhood size, and a `global-neighborhood' characteristic where within a few hops all vertices are in the same neighborhood.
These characteristics are often amalgamated together and termed the `small world' property.

\subsection{Mesh Graphs}
Mesh graphs are a naturally occurring graph class characterized by a flatter, more regular degree distribution.
Mesh graphs are often derived from physical phenomena like road networks and so new vertices tend to extend the graph outwards~\cite{Beamer2015}.
They tend to have a large diameter, a small variance in neighborhood size and a `local-neighborhood' characteristic within a few hops.
That is, only a small subset of vertices are reachable within a few hops of any given vertex.

\subsection{Synthetic  Graphs}
Real-world \textit{Scale-free} and \textit{Mesh} graphs are extremely useful in testing graph algorithms. 
They provide realistic inputs to algorithms reflecting the domain they are sourced from. 
However, there are many domains, and data is not available for all of them. 
Further, modeling theoretical performance on irregular distributions can be difficult. 
As a solution, \textit{synthetic graphs} can be generated to provide simplified, and/or synthetic graphs for testing and evaluation. 
They can be \textit{mathematically generated}, or \textit{imitatively generated}.

\subsubsection{Mathematically Generated Synthetic Graphs (Random Graphs)}
There are numerous mathematical functions that can be used to generate synthetic graphs.
Most of them exploit an existing probability distribution and generate a graph that reflects that probability distribution. 
Notable generators include the Erd\H{o}s-R\'enyi generator to create \textit{Uniform Random Graphs}~\cite{Erdhos1960}, the \textit{Random Matrix} (RMAT)~\cite{Chakrabarti2004} generator to generate scale-free graphs and the \textit{Kronecker}~\cite{Leskovec2010} generator, also for scale-free graph generation. 
Mathematically generated random graphs are useful approximations of the real world but provide a sub-optimal substitute for the irregularities of real graphs. 

\subsubsection{Imitatively Generated Synthetic Graphs (Imitative Graphs)}
Many graph datasets are not releasable for research and development because of privacy and confidentiality concerns. 
Active research in privacy preservation and synthetic graph generation seeks to statistically summarize a graph real-world graph, and then use that summary as the input to a synthetic graph generator so that the synthetic graph will have the same topological properties as the original, without the confidential information. 
Input features of \textit{Triangle Histograms, K-Hop Neighbour Histograms, Random Walk Histograms} and \textit{Number of Edges} have been shown to produce topologically similar graphs~\cite{Zahirnia2024}.

\subsection{Summary}
There is a heterogeneous distribution of graph classes.
Scale-free, mesh, mathematically generated and synthetically generated graphs are important examples of graph classes drawn from a wider spectrum of graphs. 
Each category has inherent drawbacks for graph processing, and these categories will be discussed throughout the remainder of this thesis as representatives of the `datasets' that \gai will operate on.
